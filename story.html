Her Story

One cup of tea at a time

Forty years, dozens of countries, and a lifetime of remedies — gathered the slow way, and shared the same.

Southern China · Where it began

Long before she had a face people recognized from a screen, Mei was a girl from a small town in southern China who spent more hours in her grandmother's herb garden than in the schoolyard. She learned early that a cup steeped the right way could ease a fever, that a root simmered long enough could settle a shaking cough, and that most of what a family needed to stay well was already growing quietly nearby — if someone still remembered how to use it.

Central Asia & India · The old trade roads

When she came of age, Mei did something few women of her village dared to do: she left. Not to escape, but to learn. She followed the old trade roads west through the mountains and deserts of Central Asia, sitting for weeks at a time with healers who taught her how dried roots and bitter barks could be coaxed into medicine. In northern India, she apprenticed under an Ayurvedic physician who showed her how turmeric, ashwagandha, and holy basil were not just spices but a whole system for keeping a body in balance — knowledge she carried with her for the rest of her life.

Southeast Asia · Leaves she'd never seen at home

She continued south and east through Southeast Asia, trading remedies with village healers who taught her their uses for ginger, galangal, and dozens of leaves and roots she'd never seen at home.

The Middle East & East Africa · Merchant ships

From there, merchant ships carried her further than she ever expected to go — through the Middle East, where she learned the old uses of myrrh and frankincense from apothecaries in ancient trading towns, and onward into East Africa, where healers showed her roots and barks that had eased their own families' aches for generations.

Europe · Monastery gardens, long winters

Europe came next — long winters spent in the herb gardens of old monasteries and village kitchens, learning why chamomile, valerian, and elderberry had earned their place in nearly every home on the continent.

The Americas · Plains, Andes, Amazon

And when she finally crossed the ocean, she spent years moving through the Americas: sitting with healers in the North American plains who taught her how echinacea and goldenseal had cared for their people long before anyone else arrived, then further south into the Andes and the Amazon, where remedies like maca root and cat's claw had been passed down since long before anyone kept written records at all.

Home · The kettle that never stops warming

By the time Mei finally returned home and settled into the quiet house she lives in now — the one with herbs drying from the rafters and a kettle that never seems to stop warming — she had spent the better part of four decades collecting a lifetime's worth of remedies from nearly every corner of the world. For years, she shared what she knew the old way: one guest at a time, one cup of tea at a time, one family at a time.

But Mei is not young anymore, and she has come to understand something simple and a little sad — there are only so many doors she can knock on, only so many kitchens she can sit in, before her time runs out. What she knows deserves to outlive her, and it deserves to reach further than her own two feet ever could. So, with some encouragement (and a little help getting the technology right), Grandma Mei decided to open her door to everyone at once. Now, instead of one visitor at a time, she can share what four decades and dozens of countries taught her with anyone willing to listen — starting, tonight, with something most people need and rarely get enough of: rest.
